# [HASH] — Rows tinted alternately so the zigzag still reads as QWERTY, the tint derived from the press colour rather than picked

The user's idea, and the reason is the perceptual wedge itself: the rows are still there, they only zigzag, and people read left to right — so the row is the unit that carries recognition and QWERTYUIOP is a string almost everyone knows on sight. Highlighting rows says *this is the keyboard you already use*. Highlighting columns would say nothing, because nobody reads a keyboard downwards. SPEC's familiarity principle is what this defends: the zigzag is the one thing that makes Hexboard look unfamiliar.

Tinting the circles rather than banding behind them, also the user's, and the reason is shape: a band behind a zigzagging row is not a stripe but a wave, and it would be the first non-circular thing on the board. Tinting adds no shape at all. Their brief for how far to take it: nothing crazy, just enough to notice.

How far is derived rather than chosen. `KeyboardPanel` already draws each key as `lerp(fill, lit, glow)`, resting at one colour and fully lit at another when pressed. Even rows rest at the existing fill; odd rows rest one-fifth of the way along that same line. The tint therefore introduces no new colour, cannot clash with the theme, and its distance from a pressed key is measurable rather than a matter of taste — a press travels the whole way, so a pressed key stays four-fifths brighter than its unpressed neighbours even in a tinted row. That is what keeps this clear of [key-press-feedback], which shipped that morning and makes the pressed key's lightening the only press feedback there is. The one-fifth itself is Claude's proposal and derived from nothing; it is one constant, adjustable once seen on the phone, and seeing it is the point at which to judge it.

A stepped tint — one shade per row rather than two alternating — was refused because the difference accumulates down the board, so the bottom row would sit far from the top instead of reading as banding.

This item also carries a comment into `KeyGeometry.kt`, folded in rather than filed separately: `nearestCentre` documents *that* full coverage holds and not *why* it matters. The why belongs with [uniform-neighbours-predictive], where it is recorded, and a pointer in the code is what a reader of the geometry would meet.
