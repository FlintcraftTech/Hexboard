# [HASH] — Settle key-layout.json as the source of truth for key data, split the licence/go-public item into three with its red flag cleared, and sync SPEC

This session processed four captures and answered the question the last one left open: where key data actually lives. The decision is that `resources/key-layout.json` is the single source of truth and `resources/key-manifest.md` is generated from it, not the reverse. A generated view cannot drift by construction, whereas hand-authoring the manifest and parsing it into JSON would only swap the old drift risk for a markdown-parser one. Neither file is hand-edited in practice — Claude makes the edits and Alex reviews the generated manifest in the same readable form it has today — and the eventual variant editor writes JSON far more reliably than markdown tables. Confirming `hexboard17.html` as frozen is what reduced this from three consumers to one, and made the whole thing tractable.

A second decision bounds what the config may contain: the key inventory only, with geometry staying in Kotlin. The reason is the perceptual wedge SPEC calls inviolable — if geometry were configurable, a fork could flatten the zag or square off the keys and Hexboard would stop meaning anything. A variant may change which letters you get, never how it feels to aim at them. The accepted cost is that a future per-language geometry change, such as a fourth row, becomes a code change rather than a config change; judged the right trade, since that's a deliberate design decision anyway.

That decision reshaped `[android-key-audit]`. Its original premise — compare the Kotlin keyboard's character set against the manifest — dies once the app reads the config directly, because the two then cannot disagree and the check would be trivially true. What remains worth checking split in two: whether the config itself is sound (duplicates, collisions, malformed accent lists), which needs no keyboard and was split out as `[key-config-validator]`; and whether every key in the config actually renders and emits its character, which is a real wiring bug class and kept the original slug.

`[licence-and-go-public]` was three things in one coat. After a web search — filed at `resources/research/licence-options.md` — Alex chose PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0. Nothing off-the-shelf matches his intent exactly, because that intent is a purpose-limited derivative right (fork it to build Hexboard in another language, no general right to copy) and standard licences limit by who or by commercial purpose instead. A custom licence was weighed and rejected: it would match on paper but bespoke text is often unenforceable without a lawyer and deters the contributors the project wants. Public-with-no-licence was also weighed and rejected as too high-friction for a project that wants language forks. The known gap in PolyForm Noncommercial — it permits noncommercial forks that weren't the intent — was accepted, since the realistic threat is commercial appropriation rather than a hobbyist rival.

The red flag on that item was cleared by designing the risk out rather than accepting it. The risk was that going public exposes the entire git history, not just current files, and this repo's history includes machine paths and could include personal detail. The fix is ordering: `[git-history-audit]` is now a separate audit item that reads the full history and reports findings, and `[repo-go-public]` records completion of that audit as a hard lift-condition. Nothing is exposed until the audit has run and its findings are dealt with. Going public is effectively irreversible — once history is public it can be cloned — which is why the gate is a prerequisite rather than a follow-up.

`[variant-editor]` was deliberately not designed. Its subjects don't exist yet: no keyboard, so nothing to vary; no fork, so the fork-sync question relocated into it from the licence item has no real case to reason about; and the SPEC-level question underneath it, whether Hexboard becomes a platform for variants, is better answered with a working keyboard in hand. It was sharpened with what this session settled — a concrete target format, and a scope hard-bounded away from the perceptual wedge — and left for a later planning session.
